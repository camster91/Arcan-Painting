When homeowners see a painter using a spray gun, one of two things usually happens: they get excited ("that must be faster!") or nervous ("is that going to be messy?"). Both reactions make sense. Spray finishing is a genuinely different technique from brush and roller work — with real advantages and real limitations.

## How Spray Finishing Works

A professional airless sprayer pushes paint through a small tip at high pressure, atomizing it into a fine mist that lays down in a thin, even film. The result, when done well, is a glass-smooth finish with no brush marks and no roller texture.

This is why you see spray equipment on:
- Kitchen cabinet doors and boxes
- Interior doors and trim
- New construction (before occupants move in)
- Exterior siding on large homes or commercial buildings

## The Advantages of Spray Finishing

### Perfect Smoothness

A sprayed finish, properly applied, has no texture whatsoever. You can run your hand over a sprayed cabinet door and feel nothing but the paint itself. This is simply not achievable with a brush or roller — both leave some surface evidence behind.

For furniture-quality finishes on cabinetry, built-ins, or high-gloss trim, spray is the only way to get there.

### Speed on Large Open Areas

On flat, unobstructed surfaces — exterior walls, large ceilings in empty spaces, new construction interiors — spray covers more square footage per hour than any other method. What would take a day with rollers can take a few hours with a sprayer.

### Consistent Film Thickness

A skilled sprayer lays down an even, consistent coat across the whole surface. This matters for high-gloss finishes where any inconsistency shows up as a visual variation.

## The Limitations

### Overspray Is Real

Spray equipment produces mist that travels. In a home, this means masking off everything you don't want painted — and doing it well. Floors, furniture, windows, outlets, fixtures, neighboring walls — all need protection. The masking can take as long as the spraying itself in an occupied home.

This is why spray isn't always faster in lived-in spaces. The setup overhead is significant.

### Not Always the Right Tool in Tight Spaces

Corners, behind appliances, detailed molding with lots of profiles — brush work is often more precise and controllable in these areas. A spray gun is hard to use in tight spots without overspray going everywhere.

### Skill Requirement

Spraying badly is very easy. You can get runs, uneven coverage, blush (cloudiness from humidity), and orange peel texture (small bumps from incorrect technique or viscosity). It takes real practice to spray consistently well. A professional painter with spray experience is worth paying for this specifically.

## Brush and Roller: When It's the Right Choice

Don't mistake "not spray" for "inferior." Brush and roller is the right choice for:

- **Occupied homes where masking everything isn't practical**
- **Wall painting in most residential projects** — a quality roller leaves a very slight texture that's actually desirable on walls; it hides minor imperfections and looks natural
- **Touch-up work** — you can't touch up a spray job easily with a brush and have it match
- **Exterior work on complex buildings** with lots of trim, windows, and details

Many professional painters use spray and brush/roller on the same job. They might spray the cabinet doors, back-roll the large wall areas (spray then immediately roll to work the paint in), and brush the detail trim.

## Cabinet Refinishing: The Best Use Case for Spray

If you're refinishing kitchen cabinets, spray is by far the best approach for the doors and face frames. Here's what a proper cabinet spray job looks like:

1. Doors removed and taken to a shop or laid flat
2. Surfaces sanded, deglossed, and cleaned thoroughly
3. Primer sprayed and cured
4. Light sand between coats
5. Final color coats sprayed
6. Topcoat (usually a water-based polyurethane) applied

The result is indistinguishable from factory-finished cabinetry if done well. It's a completely different outcome than brush painting cabinets in-place.

## Questions to Ask Your Painter

If a painter proposes spray work on your project, ask:
- Will you back-roll the walls? (Back-rolling — applying a roller after spraying — works paint into the surface and helps with adhesion and texture)
- How are you protecting areas not being painted?
- Do you spray the doors off-site or in-place?
- What happens if there's overspray?

A professional will have clear answers to all of these.
