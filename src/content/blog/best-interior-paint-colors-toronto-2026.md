Choosing paint colors is one of those decisions that feels small until you're staring at a freshly painted wall thinking "that's not what I expected." Light in Toronto homes is specific — we have overcast winters, east-west exposures in a lot of semi-detached homes, and older houses with smaller windows. What looks great in a California showroom can feel cold and grey here.

Here's what's actually working in Toronto interiors right now.

## The Shift Away from Grey

For about a decade, grey was the answer to every interior painting question in Toronto. Cool greys, warm greys, greige. Some of it still looks great — but the moment has passed for grey as a go-to neutral.

What's replacing it: warm whites, soft creams, earthy taupes, and deeper naturals. The shift is toward colors that feel warm under artificial light, especially in our grey winters.

## Warm Whites That Actually Work

The challenge with white is undertones. A white with a blue undertone reads as stark and cold in a north-facing Toronto room. What you want is a white with warm undertones — cream, yellow, or even the faintest blush.

**Benjamin Moore White Dove (OC-17)** — probably the most reliable warm white on the market. It's not cream, it's not stark. Works in almost any light condition.

**Benjamin Moore Chantilly Lace (OC-65)** — cleaner than White Dove, slightly cooler, but still warm enough to use throughout. Good if you want "crisp" without "clinical."

**Sherwin-Williams Alabaster (SW 7008)** — a slightly creamier off-white. Looks beautiful on trim and pairs well with almost anything on the walls.

## The Earthy Neutrals Taking Over

This is where Toronto design is moving right now — away from cool neutrals toward warm, grounded tones. Think warm sand, terracotta-adjacent beiges, and muted ochres.

**Benjamin Moore Pale Oak (OC-20)** — a perfect warm greige. Not brown, not grey, not beige — it lives right in the middle and works in virtually any room.

**Benjamin Moore Camouflage (2143-40)** — an earthy sage green with grey in it. Looks especially good in older Toronto homes with wood trim and large windows.

**Sherwin-Williams Accessible Beige (SW 7036)** — one of the most used neutrals in the GTA for a reason. It adapts to warm and cool light and feels comfortable without being boring.

## Dark and Moody: When to Go For It

Deep, saturated colours have had a real moment — navy libraries, forest green kitchens, charcoal dining rooms. This trend has legs because done right, it looks incredible.

The key: dark walls work best in rooms with specific purpose (a library, a dining room, a primary bedroom) and where you can control the lighting. They don't work as well in small, dark rooms with no natural light.

**Benjamin Moore Hale Navy (HC-154)** — a rich navy that never reads as black. Excellent for kitchens, mudrooms, or feature walls.

**Farrow & Ball Mole's Breath** — a sophisticated grey-brown that looks almost taupe in daylight and much deeper at night. For formal living rooms or dining rooms.

**Benjamin Moore Black Forest Green (2047-10)** — a deep, desaturated green. Looks stunning in a kitchen or bathroom with brass hardware.

## What Works in Canadian Light

Toronto light has specific characteristics that affect how paint reads:
- **North-facing rooms** get cool, diffuse light — avoid cool greys and blues; they'll feel cold. Lean into warm neutrals and whites with yellow undertones.
- **South-facing rooms** get strong direct light in summer — you can go cooler here. Blues and cool greys look better in these rooms.
- **East-west split** (common in semis) — morning rooms feel warm and inviting early, cool later. West rooms are gorgeous at golden hour but can feel dim in the morning.

When in doubt, test with a real sample. Paint 12×12 inch patches in the actual room, observe at different times of day, and live with it for 2–3 days before committing.

## Practical Tips Before You Pick

**Buy sample pots, not chips.** Paint chips at the hardware store are printed, not actual paint. Get the sample pot, roll it on the wall, let it dry fully (colour shifts as it dries), and look at it in both daylight and evening light.

**Consider the whole house flow.** If you're painting multiple rooms, think about how the colors flow from space to space. Open-plan homes especially need colors that work together.

**Ask about sheen.** For most walls, an eggshell (slight sheen, wipeable) is the right choice. Flat paint hides imperfections better but is hard to clean. Semi-gloss is for trim and high-humidity areas like bathrooms. The sheen affects how the color looks — more sheen means more light reflection.
