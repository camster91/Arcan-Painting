Commercial painting projects come with a different set of pressures than residential work. You can't just close the office for two weeks while painters work at their own pace. Business owners need fast turnarounds, minimal disruption, and results that hold up to heavy use. Here's what you need to know before hiring a commercial painter in Toronto.

## What Makes Commercial Painting Different

### Scheduling Around Your Business

The biggest difference is timing. A residential painter works 8–5, Monday to Friday. A commercial painter for an occupied space often has to work evenings, weekends, or overnight to avoid disrupting staff or customers.

If you're running a restaurant, retail space, or office that can't close, ask explicitly how the contractor plans to manage access and scheduling. Any serious commercial painting company will have experience with off-hours work — and will price it accordingly.

### Volume and Durability

Commercial spaces take more abuse than homes. High-traffic areas — corridors, stairwells, lobbies — need more durable finishes. This usually means:
- **Higher sheen levels** (satin or semi-gloss vs eggshell) so surfaces are easier to clean
- **Premium commercial-grade paints** designed for washability and scuff resistance
- More coats in high-traffic zones

The cheapest paint that looks fine on day one will look beat up within 6 months in a busy office or retail space. Specify what you expect to last.

### Building Code and Safety Compliance

Commercial spaces have different requirements than residential ones. Depending on the building type, you may need:
- **Low-VOC or zero-VOC paints** in occupied spaces (for air quality)
- Specific fire-retardant coatings in certain areas
- Proper prep of lead paint (common in older Toronto commercial buildings) following Ontario regulations

A commercial painting contractor should know these requirements and flag them. If they don't bring up lead testing for pre-1978 buildings, that's a concern.

## Types of Commercial Painting Projects

**Office interiors:** Walls, ceilings, trim. Usually done in phases to minimize disruption. Some companies do it floor by floor over weekends.

**Retail spaces:** Often involves feature walls, brand color matching, and tight deadlines around new openings or renovations.

**Restaurants and hospitality:** Needs to account for kitchen grease, humidity, and the visual standards expected for customer-facing spaces. Epoxy coatings in kitchen areas are common.

**Strata and condo buildings:** Common areas — lobbies, hallways, parkades. These projects require coordination with building management and often have strict rules about working hours and waste disposal.

**Industrial:** Warehouses, factories, and facilities. Often involves specialty coatings for concrete floors, metal structures, or exterior cladding.

## What to Include in a Commercial Painting Quote

Any commercial quote should specify:

1. **Scope of work** — exactly what gets painted (walls, ceilings, trim, doors, specific areas)
2. **Products** — paint brand, sheen level, and number of coats
3. **Surface prep** — what's included (washing, patching, priming)
4. **Schedule** — work hours, phases, and timeline
5. **Insurance** — liability coverage appropriate for commercial work
6. **WSIB** — workers' compensation coverage (this is non-negotiable)

Don't sign a contract that doesn't include the above. In commercial work especially, unclear scope leads to disputes and overruns.

## Ballpark Pricing for Commercial Painting in Toronto

Commercial pricing is harder to generalize than residential because the variables are wide. But rough guidance:

- **Small office or retail unit (1,000 sq ft):** $2,500 – $5,000
- **Mid-size office (3,000–5,000 sq ft):** $7,000 – $15,000
- **Large floor or full building:** $20,000+

Add 20–40% for after-hours or weekend work. It costs more, but it costs less than closing for a week.

## Tips for Managing a Commercial Painting Project

**Appoint an internal point of contact.** Someone on your team needs to be available to answer questions and give access. Don't expect the painting crew to manage around an empty building without guidance.

**Phase the work if you can.** Painting one section at a time while staff works in another area is usually possible and often much cheaper than doing the whole space at once.

**Get the colour right before you start.** Changing your mind mid-job is expensive. Paint samples on actual walls, review at different times of day, and get sign-off from decision-makers before the crew starts.

**Plan for smell.** Even low-VOC paint has some odour. Increase ventilation for 24–48 hours after painting. If you have staff with chemical sensitivities, let them know in advance.
