The difference between a paint job that looks great for 10 years and one that starts showing problems in 2 years almost always comes down to preparation. The paint itself matters, but prep is the foundation everything sits on.

Here's how to do it properly.

## Step 1: Clear and Protect the Space

Before you touch the walls, protect everything else:
- Move furniture to the center of the room and cover with drop sheets
- Remove outlet covers and light switch plates (tape them to the outlet/switch so you know where they go back)
- Lay canvas drop sheets on the floor — not plastic, which is slippery and lets paint seep through at the edges
- Tape off baseboards, window frames, and door casings if you're not painting them

## Step 2: Clean the Walls

Paint doesn't stick well to dirty surfaces. This step gets skipped constantly, and it shows.

**What to clean:**
- **Kitchen walls:** Grease from cooking settles on walls, even far from the stove. Wipe down with TSP (trisodium phosphate) solution or a degreaser, then rinse.
- **Bathroom walls:** Soap scum and moisture residue. Same treatment.
- **Living areas:** Usually just dust, but still worth a wipe with a damp cloth.

Let walls dry completely before moving to the next step. This is especially important for kitchens and bathrooms.

## Step 3: Fix What's Broken

This is the real work. Take time here and everything else is easier.

**Small nail holes and dents:** Use lightweight spackle. Apply with a putty knife, overfill slightly, let dry, sand smooth. For very small holes, some painters use toothpaste in a pinch — it works, but spackle is better.

**Larger holes (up to about 6 inches):** Use a self-adhesive mesh patch plus joint compound (also called mud). Apply the patch, spread a thin coat of mud over it, feather out the edges, let dry, sand, apply a second coat if needed.

**Hairline cracks:** Common in older Toronto homes due to seasonal movement. Fill with lightweight spackle or a flexible paintable caulk. Rigid filler in active cracks will just crack again.

**Larger cracks or structural cracks:** If a crack keeps coming back or is wider than a hairline, it may indicate a structural issue. Get it looked at before just painting over it.

**Popped drywall screws:** You'll see a small bump or circle on the wall. Drive a new screw about 2 inches away to anchor the drywall, then countersink the old screw (drive it in just below the surface). Cover both with spackle and sand.

## Step 4: Sand

After any patching, sand the repaired areas smooth with 120-grit sandpaper. You're aiming for a seamless transition between the patch and the surrounding wall — run your hand over it and feel for ridges.

If the walls have significant texture inconsistency (common in older homes where multiple layers of paint have been applied), a light sanding of the whole wall with a pole sander will help the new paint lay more evenly.

**Always wipe down sanded areas** with a slightly damp cloth to remove dust before painting. Sanding dust prevents adhesion.

## Step 5: Deal with Stains

Not all stains block with regular primer. Water stains, smoke damage, and marker all need a shellac-based primer (like Zinsser BIN) or an oil-based primer to seal them. If you paint latex directly over a water stain, the stain bleeds through — often through multiple coats.

Spot-prime the stain first. Let it dry completely. Then proceed with your regular primer and paint.

## Step 6: Primer — When Do You Need It?

You don't always need a separate primer coat. But you do when:
- **Painting bare drywall or fresh patches** — raw drywall absorbs paint unevenly, creating a "flashing" effect (some areas dull, some shiny). Primer seals it.
- **Changing from dark to light** — a grey tinted primer cut specifically for your new color makes the transition in fewer coats.
- **Dealing with stains** — as above.
- **Painting over a glossy finish** — scuff sand first, then prime for adhesion.

If your walls are in reasonable shape and you're painting a similar or darker colour, a quality self-priming paint is often enough.

## Step 7: Caulk the Gaps

Before painting, run a bead of paintable caulk along any gaps between the wall and trim (baseboards, door casings, window frames). This gives a clean finished look and prevents drafts and moisture from getting behind the trim.

Use a damp finger to smooth the caulk bead. Let it dry before painting. Latex caulk typically dries in 1–2 hours.

## What Happens If You Skip the Prep?

- **Skipped cleaning:** Paint peels within a year, especially in kitchens and bathrooms.
- **Unfilled holes:** Every imperfection shows through the new paint — often more than before.
- **Unstained stains:** Bleed-through shows up immediately or within weeks.
- **No primer on bare drywall:** The finish looks splotchy and uneven even with multiple coats.

Professional painters spend roughly a third of their time on prep. It's not the exciting part, but it's what makes the difference.
